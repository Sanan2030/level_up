## Learning objectives

- Describe a job in terms of situation and desired progress.
- Interview about a real event without suggesting an answer.
- Convert findings into testable product hypotheses.

## Why this matters

Two people of the same age and profession can choose different products because their situations differ. A customer may order groceries to avoid carrying bags after a late shift, while another is preparing for guests. Research that asks only which features people like can miss the progress they are trying to make.

## Core theory

Jobs to Be Done examines why people adopt a product or alternative to make progress in particular circumstances. Functional, emotional and social dimensions can all matter. A job statement should describe the situation and desired progress without embedding your preferred product as the only solution.

Investigate a real recent event. Ask what was happening, what triggered action, what alternatives were considered and how the person decided. Include the old method, delay and doing nothing as alternatives. A timeline helps distinguish what the person actually did from a general opinion about what they might do.

Use open prompts and neutral follow-ups. “What happened the last time you needed groceries after work?” invites evidence. “Would automatic reordering make life easier?” suggests a feature and encourages hypothetical agreement. Probe trade-offs: what was inconvenient, what caused concern and what nearly prevented the choice?

Recruit relevant participants beyond your happiest paying customers. People who abandoned, switched away or used a competing method can expose different constraints. Obtain appropriate consent and protect sensitive examples. A small qualitative sample can develop explanations and hypotheses; it does not establish how common each motivation is in the entire market.

Synthesize repeated patterns while preserving contradictory cases and context. Keep observed events separate from your interpretation. Express an opportunity and the evidence that could change it. A proposed solution then needs validation through suitable prototypes, behavior or experiments, not a claim that interview enthusiasm proves demand.

## Worked example

A researcher interviews a nurse who ordered groceries after a late shift. The triggering event was discovering that home supplies were missing before an early start. The alternatives were a nearby shop, asking a family member and delivery. The customer chose delivery despite a higher price because the arrival window was clear and carrying bags was difficult.

The emerging job is about obtaining essentials reliably under time and energy constraints. “Build a subscription” is only one possible response. The team records a hypothesis that a trustworthy short delivery window matters more than a large promotional discount for this situation. It plans a small test instead of generalizing the interview to every shopper.

## Inspect the decision

<details>
<summary>Five interviewees say they like a proposed feature. Is demand established?</summary>

No. Hypothetical approval is weaker than evidence of an actual problem, choice and behavior. Examine the interview wording, sample and alternatives. Test a clearly stated hypothesis with an appropriate observable outcome before assuming adoption or willingness to pay.

</details>

## Common mistakes

- Defining a job as “use our app.”
- Treating a persona’s demographic label as a complete explanation.
- Replacing recent-event questions with feature voting.
- Presenting qualitative patterns as market-wide percentages.

## Practice exercise

Write five neutral questions about the last time a customer changed a subscription. Include the trigger, previous alternative, decision process, concern and result.

## Worked solution

<details>
<summary>Show worked solution</summary>

Ask what happened before the change, how the person previously met the need, what they considered, what nearly stopped them and what happened afterward. Follow the chronology and request concrete examples where appropriate. Record observations and interpretations separately, then write a hypothesis about desired progress that does not require your preferred feature to be true.

</details>

## Check understanding and cheat sheet

Situation + desired progress + alternatives + real choice + outcome. Use interviews to understand and form hypotheses, then validate the product response.
