## Concept

A Scrum Team is structured and empowered to manage its work. It collectively has or acquires the capabilities needed for product-related activity. Requiring every everyday decision to pass through several external managers, or spreading people across many initiatives, can undermine that autonomy. The supplied Professional Scrum Competencies text identifies Organizational Design and Culture, Portfolio Planning and Evidence-Based Management as areas of organizational agility.

## Why it matters

Chapter 8 of the book describes how parallel projects compete for people’s time and delay completion. Starting more work does not necessarily finish more value. Portfolio decisions need visibility of why products receive investment, available capacity and dependencies. The book’s capacity examples are management guidance; the Scrum Guide does not prescribe a universal product-to-team ratio. Avoid turning an example into a context-free rule.

## Decision principles

Chapter 3 of the book explains how rewards and punishments tied to a measure can distort information. Requiring every team to produce more story points can inflate estimates without shortening delivery time to customers. EBM 2024 examines experienced and unmet value through CV and UV, and delivery capability through T2M and A2I. It does not require combining everything into one score.

## Worked scenario

An organization assigns the same specialists to six product initiatives. Every status report is green, but no usable result reaches users for months. Leadership wants to compare team velocities and penalize the lowest performer.

## Decision reasoning

Before blaming individuals or teams, make the system visible: how many initiatives are open, where work waits, who must decide, and which outcomes reached customers? Compare investments against product goals. Test less parallel work and faster decisions for one small value stream. The Scrum Master helps remove organizational barriers, while the Product Owner contributes evidence about investment and product outcomes.

## Common pitfalls

Pitfalls include using velocity as a cross-team performance ranking, responding to every delay with more people, equating self-management with directionless freedom, and declaring every portfolio item equally urgent. An organizational change is also a hypothesis needing purpose and inspection; no structure can simply be assumed to work everywhere.

## Application exercise

Write four questions for leadership and one organizational experiment. Explain how the experiment could benefit customers as well as make work easier for teams.

## Explained solution

<details>
<summary>Show explained solution</summary>

Possible questions: which outcome matters most, how much work is open, where is the largest wait, and which decisions can the team make? Experiment by reducing specialist fragmentation and clarifying decision boundaries for one product journey. Inspect usable-change delivery time and customer outcomes. Fewer meetings alone do not prove success; adapt if waiting and benefit do not improve.

</details>

## Check understanding and remember

Portfolio: which outcome receives investment? Culture: can people safely reveal bad news? Autonomy: are purpose and boundaries clear? Measurement: how does evidence improve decisions?
