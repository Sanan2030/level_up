## Concept

In Scrum, an Increment is more than separately completed components. New work must be verified together with earlier Increments and be usable. The Definition of Done creates a shared understanding of product quality. Multiple Scrum Teams working on the same product mutually define and follow the same Definition of Done. When organizational standards exist, those standards are the minimum rather than an optional target.

## Why it matters

A Product Owner does not prescribe the technical implementation, but needs to understand how quality affects customers and future investment. Acceptance criteria may describe the expected behavior of one item; they do not replace the shared Definition of Done. An export that works functionally is not Done if a mandatory security check fails. The whole Scrum Team is accountable for a valuable, useful Increment each Sprint; adding a separate final quality phase does not resolve that accountability.

## Decision principles

Chapter 3 of the book explains how technical debt can make future changes slower. Done does not necessarily mean no technical debt exists; it does mean work satisfies the shared quality definition. EBM 2024 uses A2I to examine constraints on new capabilities and T2M to examine delivery and learning speed. Continuous integration and automated checks can help, but Scrum prescribes no particular tool, delivery pipeline or programming method.

## Worked scenario

Three teams build the interface, calculation and audit parts of a payments product. Each reports completion, but integrated checks show inconsistent amounts. A sponsor asks to postpone the audit check to preserve the promised date.

## Decision reasoning

First make the product state transparent: component completion is not an integrated Done Increment. Do not change quality criteria merely to fit the date. Collaborate with Developers on the cause and a smaller usable scope. The Product Owner explains value and ordering trade-offs; Developers own the technical plan. Earlier integration and smaller changes can be tested as improvements in subsequent work.

## Common pitfalls

Common mistakes include assigning quality only to testers, allowing incompatible Done definitions, assuming all technical debt means unfinished work, and using local output as proof of product value. A technical proposal also needs an explanation of impact: not every refactoring is automatically the most valuable next investment.

## Application exercise

Write three observations and one small improvement experiment for the scenario. Include an observation about product quality and another about the ability to make future changes. What result would show that the experiment was not useful?

## Explained solution

<details>
<summary>Show explained solution</summary>

One answer: expose integration failures, waiting time for integration, and usable changes that pass security checks. Test integrating smaller changes daily for one product journey. That cadence is a teaching example, not a Scrum rule. If waiting does not fall or production failures rise, investigate instead of declaring automation successful.

</details>

## Check understanding and remember

Shared Done → integrated evidence → usable Increment. Item-specific acceptance criteria do not waive shared quality. Explain technical investment through the capability to deliver future value.
